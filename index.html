What is ADA?

ADA stands for the Americans with Disabilities Act, a federal civil rights law that prohibits discrimination against people with disabilities — including how they access information and services online.

What this means for this site

  • We build to WCAG (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines) standards
  • Real, functional accessibility tools — text resizing, high contrast, underline links — not just a statement page

HIPAA

What is HIPAA?

HIPAA stands for the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act — a federal law that sets standards for protecting sensitive patient health information. It governs how that information can be collected, stored, transmitted, and disclosed.

What this means for your website

If your practice's website collects any patient information — even something as simple as a contact form — how that data is handled matters. We build with HIPAA considerations from the ground up: minimizing unnecessary data collection, using secure transmission, and being thoughtful about where information is stored.

What is a BAA?

A BAA (Business Associate Agreement) is a legal contract required under HIPAA between a healthcare provider ("covered entity") and any vendor ("business associate") that may handle protected health information on their behalf. If a tool or service used on your site could touch patient data, a valid BAA should be in place with that vendor — this is part of making your practice's digital presence genuinely compliant, not just compliant-looking.

This information is educational and does not constitute legal advice. Consult a healthcare compliance attorney for guidance specific to your practice.

Attorney-Client Privilege

What is Attorney-Client Privilege?

Attorney-Client Privilege is a legal protection that keeps communications between a lawyer and client confidential — they generally cannot be forced into court or disclosed to the other side. Every attorney should already know this one; it's foundational.

Duty of Confidentiality — State Bar Rule 1.6

Less discussed, but broader: the Duty of Confidentiality (based on ABA Model Rule 1.6, adopted with variations by each state bar) requires attorneys to protect all information related to representing a client — not just direct communications. Rule 1.6(c) specifically requires "reasonable efforts to prevent the inadvertent or unauthorized disclosure of, or unauthorized access to" client information. This is the ethics-based equivalent of what HIPAA requires for healthcare data — enforced through state bar discipline rather than federal law.

Where this matters for your website

Any intake form, client portal, or communication tool on your site touches information covered by this duty. We build with that in mind — secure handling, minimal unnecessary data collection, and no sharing of submitted information with third parties.

A note for accident, malpractice & workers' comp attorneys

If your practice regularly receives medical records as part of a case, your firm may qualify as a HIPAA "Business Associate" — meaning HIPAA itself can apply directly to your firm, requiring a signed BAA with any vendor touching that data. Worth confirming with a compliance attorney if this applies to your practice.
